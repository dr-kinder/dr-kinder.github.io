A-Level Exams

What to expect

To argue for an A in this class, you need a compelling case for a C or better and you need to pass one A-level exam for each unit (C and N).

An A-level exam is your chance to show that you can think like a physicist. It is optional, it is challenging, and you can try as many times as you like.

Format

  • There are two formats, with the same standard. In a chalk talk, you solve an unseen problem at the chalkboard, in conversation with me. In a written exam, you solve an unseen problem with pencil and paper.
  • You can attempt an exam more than once, with a different problem each time. You only need to pass once.
  • Schedule a time during office hours. Do not wait until Week 10.
  • No notes, no Google, no AI — just you, your brain, and a pencil or a piece of chalk.

What I Am Looking For

You need to demonstrate thinking like a physicist, not just algebra, calculus, and trig.

I am evaluating your knowledge of physics concepts and your problem-solving technique. You can get the “wrong answer” and still pass — if you recognize what went wrong or see that the result makes no sense. You can get “the right answer” and not pass — if you pull a formula out of thin air without understanding what it is or why it applies.

What I Am Not Testing

I am not testing recall of equations. If you identify the concepts and develop a plan, but cannot remember something like the moment of inertia of a solid disk, that is fine. I will provide it. The important thing is knowing what you need, why you need it, and where it fits into your plan.

If a problem requires a calculator, you can borrow mine — after you have solved with symbols and estimated the answer. Solve with symbols, estimate, then calculate.

How to Solve It

Make sure the steps of the problem-solving heuristic are clear.

  1. Understand the problem. Tell the story: what is happening, what is interacting, what is given, and what is being asked. Sketch it. Identify variables and unknowns. Decide what matters and what you can ignore.
  2. Make a plan. Name the principles you will use, and explain why they apply. Map out a clear path to the solution.
  3. Solve the problem. Work with symbols first. Check dimensions as you go. Plug in numbers at the end.
  4. Look back. Does the result make sense? Check the units, the size of the number, a limiting case, or make a quick estimate. If something is off, figure out what.

How to Pass

I use the same criteria to evaluate your work on the chalkboard or on paper.

Step Pass Not yet
Understand You describe the situation in physical terms. You identify what is given and what is asked. Your sketch or story captures the essential physics, and you state your assumptions. You start with an equation. You miss or misread something essential. Your assumptions are missing, or they contradict the problem.
Plan You name the principle(s) and explain why they apply. Your plan is a sequence of steps that someone else could follow. Equations appear with no reason. You name a principle that does not apply, or you do not say why it does. The plan is “plug numbers into formulas until something comes out.”
Solve You work mostly with symbols and check dimensions. If you make a slip in algebra or arithmetic, you find it and work through it. You make errors that you do not notice, or do not fix. You substitute numbers too early. You ignore units.
Look back You evaluate your answer — units, magnitude, a limit, or an estimate — and you revise if something is off. You stop at the number. You accept an implausible result without comment.

To pass, you need to show me your thought process in all four steps. Everyone makes mistakes, and I do not hold them against you, as long as you find them and work through them. A wrong number with strong reasoning and a clear-eyed look back can pass. A correct number with no reasoning behind it does not.

If you don’t pass, we’ll talk about what to work on, and you can try again later with a new problem.

In a Chalk Talk

  • Think out loud and write more than math on the board. Explain your work.
  • I will ask questions as you work. That is normal. It is how I see your reasoning.
  • Being stuck is fine. Tell me what you are considering and why.

In a Written Exam

  • Write more than math. Use words and draw pictures. Explain your reasoning.
  • Being stuck is fine. Use scratch paper. Ask me questions.

How to Prepare

  • Modeling and Rich Context problems in Six Ideas are good preparation.
  • For every problem, practice telling the story, naming the principle and why it applies, estimating before you calculate, and looking back.
  • Describe your solution out loud, or explain it to a classmate.
