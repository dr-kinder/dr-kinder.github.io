Learning Objectives

By the end of this course, you should be able to:

  • Think like a physicist. Apply relativity and quantum mechanics to explain, analyze, and predict real phenomena.
  • Think critically. Identify where relativity and quantum mechanics defy common sense, and trust evidence over instinct. Nature is always right.
  • Solve problems. Apply problem-solving heuristics in relativity and quantum mechanics. Communicate your reasoning clearly, on paper and at the board.
  • Connect theory to experiment and application. Describe key experiments that shaped modern physics. Explain technologies — MRI, GPS, semiconductors — that depend on them today.

Format

This course is co-taught by David and Jesse.

Physics education research is consistent on one point — you learn physics by doing it, not by watching someone talk about it — so most of our time together is discussion, problem solving, and chalk talks at the board, not lecture.

Come prepared: read the assigned material before class, and bring pencil, a calculator, and your laptop or tablet.

We will use a leapfrog method as we work through the text.

  • Read the chapter and attempt a few practice problems before class.
  • Ask questions. Discuss and apply the new concepts during class.
  • Attempt challenging problems after class.
  • Repeat.

Grading

There are no exams in this course. Your grade comes from problem sets, chalk talks, and two projects.

Component Percentage
Problem Sets 50
Chalk Talks 15
Midterm Project 15
Final Project 20

Problem Sets include practice problems due before class each day and challenge problems due each week. Solutions to the challenge problems will be available for self-assessment and corrections. Most of your grade on problem sets (30 points) comes from completion. The remaining points will be assigned at the end of the term by looking at the overall quality of your body of work: effort, clarity, accuracy, critical thinking, and reflection.

Chalk talks. Much of class time is discussion — of problems, of your questions. You’ll occasionally share your own work informally at a chalkboard. Regular attendance and visible, steady effort earns full credit here. You don’t need the right answer to get credit for a chalk talk. There’s a difference between being confused and being unprepared, though.

Midterm project. Research a key experiment in the development of modern physics and present it as we discuss the topic in class. These will be spread throughout the term.

Final project. Research a technology application of relativity or quantum physics. Prepare a written report and peer-review classmates’ reports.

Academic Integrity

Work with others, use outside resources, learn from peers and tutors — but submit your own original work. What you turn in should show your own reasoning, your own calculations, your own conclusions. Generative AI is useful for composing text and as a tutor — you are welcome to use it. But don’t ask it to do your assignments for you. You do the thinking, you ask the questions. If AI contributes substantially to your work, acknowledge it, like any other source you would cite.

Presenting someone else’s work — human or AI — as your own is academic dishonesty. Confirmed plagiarism or cheating earns zero credit for the assignment in question, and reporting to the Student Affairs Office is mandatory. Review Oregon Tech’s Student Academic Integrity Policy if you’re not already familiar with it.
